Laser Amplification

The amplification of a laser occurs due to the doubling of photons in each stimulated emission, resulting in an increase in light intensity. The diagram depicts how a single photon can produce eight photons through stimulated emission, demonstrating the amplification process. It should be noted that the seven atoms illustrated in the diagram must already be in an excited state. However, the impact of spontaneous emission, which will be explored later, is negligible.

Stimulated emission

De-excitation can take place via a different mechanism, where the electron doesn’t randomly drop to the ground state, but instead can be triggered by an incoming photon of the correct energy. This occurs when the oscillating force of the electromagnetic field from the incoming photon acts on the excited electron, causing it to descend.

During this process, the emitted photon, when the electron drops to the ground state, shares the same frequency, phase, and direction as the incoming photon. The de-excitation of an atom, due to the incoming photon, produces a second photon in stimulated emission, as shown in the diagram. The energy of photons responsible for stimulated emission is also capable of causing excitation (absorption). When a material is in thermal equilibrium, its atoms occupy various energy levels in a statistical distribution, but the majority of atoms are in the ground state with only a few in an excited state. Although the probability of absorption and stimulated emission for a single atom is considered equal, the likelihood of absorption is significantly higher than that of stimulated emission due to the vast majority of atoms being in the ground state. To increase the probability of stimulated emission, a greater number of atoms in the excited state compared to those in the ground state is required.
