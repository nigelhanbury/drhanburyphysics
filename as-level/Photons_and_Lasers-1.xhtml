2.7 Photons

In the 1800’s, experiments were performed which conclusively demonstrated that light was a wave. This was important as Newton considered light to be made up of particles or corpuscules. The observations that demonstrated that light behaved as waves is shown below.

Diffraction: Light spreads out as it passes through a narrow slit.

Interference: Young’s double slit experiment, light constructively and destructively interferes.

Polarization: Shows that light is a transverse wave.

Particle theory could not explain these phenomena.

By the late nineteenth century, the wave model of light was well established and accepted. However, Max Planck and Einstein revisited the particle model. The photoelectric effect was a fundamental demonstration of the particle model of light.

The diagram on the right represents the apparatus used to demonstrate the photoelectric effect.

A clean zinc plate is placed on top of the gold leaf electroscope.

The electroscope is given a negative charge.

When bright (visible) light is incident on the zinc, the leaf does not fall. The electroscope remains charged. Diagram A, depicts the use of green light.

Diagram B, depicts ultraviolet radiation incident on the zinc and the gold leaf falls, demonstrating that the electroscope discharges.

For the electroscope to discharge, electrons must have been emitted from the surface of the zinc. These electrons are referred to as photoelectrons.

The Photoelectric Effect

Observations

The electrons emitted immediately - no time lag.

Increasing the intensity of the light increases the number of photoelectrons per unit time, but not their maximum kinetic energy.

Red light will not cause the ejection of electrons, no matter what the intensity.

A weak violet light will eject only a few electrons, but their maximum kinetic energies are greater than those for more intense light of lower frequency.
