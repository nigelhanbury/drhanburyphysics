Spectral Intensity Graph for Absorption

The diagram on the right, represents the absorption spectrum showing the presence of hydrogen in the star’s atmosphere. Additionally, it shows the spectral intensity variation across the visible spectrum. As can be seen, dips (local minima) within the spectral intensity correspond to absorption lines. It is important to point out that absorption can take place outside of the visible spectrum, and in this instance, the absorption spectrum can only be viewed via the spectral intensity dips.

Notice that the spectral intensity does not reduce to zero for the absorption lines. When specific wavelengths are absorbed by the atoms due to atomic excitation, the atoms rapidly de-excite and re-emit the same wavelengths in all directions. Therefore, the spectral intensity of wavelengths re-emitted in the forward direction towards Earth is much smaller, but not zero.

Wien’s Displacement Law

Previously, we discussed how the position of the peak spectral intensity changes for increasing temperature. In fact, the wavelength of the peak intensity is found to be inversely proportional to the absolute temperature in Kelvin (K) of the body or λ  max ∝  1 _ T  .

Alternatively, λ  max =   W _ T  , where W represents Wien’s constant, W = 2.9 × 10  -3 mK .

Properties of Black Body Curves

• They start from zero (even when the curve stays close to the x-axis)

• They do not cross each other

• Have a sharp rise and a slow fall

• Continue asymptotically along the wavelength (x) axis without crossing or touching the axis

• Peak shifts left (wavelength decreases) for increasing temperature

• Are labelled for the temperature producing the emitted radiation

• Gradient approaches zero for very small and large wavelengths

Definition of Wien’s Displacement Law

The wavelength of peak emission from a black body is inversely proportional to the absolute (kelvin) temperature of the body. This is Wien’s law.
