Motion along a Straight Line – Motion Graphs

When producing motion graphs, we are only concerned with motion along one dimension (or direction). The diagram on the right, represents a body moving along the x direction. Here, positive displacement is chosen to be towards the right, and similarly for velocity and acceleration.

The diagram below, represents a displacement-time graph with its corresponding velocity-time graph. Remembering that instantaneous velocity is defined as the rate of change of displacement, it follows that the gradient of the tangent of a displacement-time graph will give the instantaneous velocity. The linear (straight) regions of the graph, A – B, C – D, E – F, represent constant velocity as the gradient between these points is constant. E – F, has a negative gradient, and hence, a negative velocity indicating that the object is travelling to the left in the negative direction. The remaining regions are non-linear, and on close inspection of the graph, the gradient of the tangent is seen to change for increasing time, and hence, the velocity is not constant.

The area between the line (or curve) and the time axis of a velocity-time graph represents the displacement. The first shaded area above, represents the total displacement up to point C on the graph. The second shaded region represents the decrease in displacement from point D to point G on the graph. The reason for this, is that the area under the time axis is negative, and hence, represents a negative displacement between these two points. Since the final displacement at point G is zero, it follows that the total area under the graph must also be zero. The two areas discussed must be equal and opposite. From a physical point of view, the object achieves a maximum positive displacement at point C followed by the vector addition of a negative displacement at point D. Therefore, the vector sum of the two displacements is zero (The object ends at the starting point).
