Resistivity

Consider diagram 1 to the right. This shows two conductors made of the same material but conductor B has double the cross-sectional area of conductor A. Both conductors have the same p.d. applied across them. Conductor B has twice the number of free charge carriers (free electrons) as its volume is doubled. Since the mean drift velocity of the free electrons are equal in both conductors, there must be twice as much charge passing any cross-sectional area per unit time for conductor B. Therefore, for every free electron in conductor A that has a mean drift velocity, there are two in conductor B. Therefore, the current flowing through conductor B must be double that flowing through conductor A. In short, doubling the cross-sectional area, doubles the current. Since both conductors have the same p.d. applied across them and conductor B carries twice the current, conductor B must have half the resistance of conductor A.

Consider diagram 2 above. To understand how the resistance changes with length, we need to remember what potential difference means. The p.d. across the conductor of length, L, is V volts, meaning that V joules of electrical potential energy is converted to other forms (thermal in this case) for every 1 coulomb of charge that passes through length, L. This energy is transferred evenly within the conductor. Therefore, exactly half of the electrical potential energy is converted to other forms for every 1 coulomb that passes through half the length of the conductor, ½ L. This means that the p.d. across the length, ½ L, must be ½ V.
