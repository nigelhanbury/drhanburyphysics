Laser Production

When a population inversion is attained, a photon with energy E2 - E1 will be produced due to spontaneous emission from state (U) to state (L). This photon then induces stimulated emission of state (U), triggering a cascade effect that generates an increasing number of photons. From this point on, stimulated emission dominates the de-excitation of atoms in state (U), while spontaneous emission can be deemed insignificant in comparison.

Efficiency

Upon examination of the diagram, it is evident that the energy of the pumping photons, E3 - E1, exceeds that of the photons produced via stimulated emission, E2 - E1. As a result, the wavelength of the pumping light will be shorter than that of the laser light created and will not interfere with the stimulated emission. To maintain the population inversion, the rate of excitation from state (L) to (P) must be equal to the rate of stimulated emission from state (U) to (L); otherwise, the population of atoms in state (U) will decline, causing a loss of population inversion. Consequently, the pumping energy delivery rate will be greater than the laser light energy production rate, leading to a significant amount of thermal energy wastage within the system. The explanation behind this is that the transition from state (P) to (U) does not emit a photon; rather, the difference in energy between the two levels is transferred to the surroundings by raising the kinetic energy of the atoms. As a result, this type of laser functions at a very high temperature and necessitates substantial cooling.
