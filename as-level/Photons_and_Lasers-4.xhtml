The Photocell

The vacuum photocell is a glass tube that contains a metal plate with a photo-emissive material, referred to as the cathode or photocathode. A smaller metal electrode referred to as the anode collects the photoelectrons emitted from the cathode. When light exceeding the threshold frequency of the metal is directed at the cathode, electrons are emitted from the surface. A micro-ammeter in the circuit measures the photoelectric current. This current is proportional to the number of electrons per second that transfer from the cathode to the anode.

Measuring Maximum Kinetic Energy

A photocell is connected in the circuit shown, with the cathode connected to the positive terminal of the power supply. With the output of the power supply set to zero and provided that the frequency of the incident light is greater than the threshold frequency of the cathode metal, the micro-ammeter should register that a current is flowing.

As the voltage output of the power supply is increased, the current registered on the micro-ammeter decreases as the number of photoelectrons reaching the anode per second decreases. The voltage at which the current just drops to zero is called the stopping voltage, VS. At this voltage, not even those photoelectrons with the maximum kinetic energy reach the anode. As the photoelectrons move away from the cathode their kinetic energy is converted into electric potential energy (a little like throwing a ball upwards where its kinetic energy is converted to gravitational potential energy). When those photoelectrons emitted with the maximum kinetic energy lose all their kinetic energy before reaching the anode, the current drops to zero. At this point, all the kinetic energy has been converted into electrical potential energy. The numerical value of VS is equal to Ekmax in units of eV. Therefore, Ekmax = VS (eV).

It is important that the positive terminal is connected to the cathode and the negative terminal connected to the anode.
