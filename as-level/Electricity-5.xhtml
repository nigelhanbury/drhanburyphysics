Applications of Mean Drift Velocity

The above formula was derived in the context of free electrons moving through a conductor. It can, however, be applied more generally. For example, in electrolytic solutions, the charge carriers are ions e.g. in a copper sulphate solution with copper electrodes, the charge carriers are positive copper and negative sulphate ions. In this case, e, would be replaced by q, as q would represent the charge on the ion.

More generally, the number density refers to the number of free charge carriers per unit volume, SI unit m-3.

Conductors (e.g. metals) are materials that have a high number density of charge carriers. n is typically of the order 1028 m-3 and hence have a low resistivity (see later)

Insulators are materials that have a very low number density of charge carriers. Insulators strongly resist the flow of charge.

Semiconductors (e.g. silicon & germanium) are materials that have a number density of charge carriers midway between those of conductors and insulators. n is typically of the order 1017 m-3 and hence have a much higher (many orders of magnitude higher) resistivity than a conductor.

Combining Conductors of Different Cross-Sectional Areas

Diagram 1 shows 3 identical conductors connected in parallel with equal cross-sectional area, A. This combination is then connected in series to a fourth conductor with a cross-sectional area 3 times greater. All conductors are made out of identical material. Here, the mean drift velocity of the free electrons through each conductor are equal.

Diagram 2 shows 2 identical conductors made out of the same material connected in series. One conductor has twice the cross-sectional area of the other. Here, the mean drift velocity of the free electrons through the larger conductor is half that of the smaller conductor.
