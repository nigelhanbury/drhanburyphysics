Experiment to Determine Internal Resistance

Setup the circuit shown on the right.

Adjust the variable resistor, R to its highest setting and record the terminal p.d., V and current, I.

Adjust the variable resistor to a lower setting and record V and I.

Repeat the above, ensuring the current values are roughly equally spaced.

Plot a graph of terminal p.d., V on the y-axis and current, I on the x-axis.

Analysis

Rearranging the equation V = E - Ir as V = -rI + E and comparing to y = mx + c, produces the following:

Gradient, m = - r and intercept, c = E, the EMF.

The magnitude of the gradient is equal to the internal resistance.

The results should look like the graph on the right. As the current through the cell increases, the terminal p.d. decreases.

Maximum current, Imax occurs when the resistance of the variable resistor, R = 0. This is equivalent to connecting the terminals of the cell together with a wire and creating a short circuit. At this point, the total resistance of the circuit is the internal resistance of the cell.

We need to be able to explain why the terminal p.d., V decreases for increasing current. In order for the current to increase, the resistance of the variable resistor, R needs to decrease as this reduces the total resistance of the circuit. As R decreases, R represents a smaller proportion of the total resistance, and therefore, takes a smaller proportion of the supply EMF. Alternatively, as the current increases, the p.d. across the internal resistance increases, resulting in a smaller p.d. across the variable resistor, as the sum of the p.d.s must be constant, which is equal to the terminal p.d.

Determining EMF

From the graph, we can see that the EMF can be determined from the intercept where the current is zero. Alternatively, a high resistance voltmeter can be connected directly across the cell terminals as shown on the right.

The terminal p.d. is equal to the EMF when there is zero current passing through the cell or battery.
