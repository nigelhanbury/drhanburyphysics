Producing Polarised Light from Natural Light

Natural light is unpolarised, meaning the oscillations (variations of the electric field) are in any direction perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation. The diagram below, shows unpolarised light entering the polarising filter. As can be seen, there are multiple directions of oscillation. Light from most sources, (lamps, flames, the sun, etc.) is unpolarised.

Only oscillations parallel to the transmission axis can pass through the polarising filter. Therefore, polarised light, with only one direction of oscillation, emerges from the filter with the direction of oscillation parallel to the transmission axis.

Investigation of the Intensity Variation for Polarisation

Initially, the polarisers are setup so that their transmission axis are parallel, which occurs when the light intensity of the emerging light is maximum. Once setup, the following observations will be made.

As the polarizing filter B rotates about its axis from 0º to 360º the intensity of the emerging light gradually decreases, until at 90º no light emerges. The intensity then gradually increases until it is again maximum at 180º. The intensity then decreases to zero at 270º and again increases to a maximum at 360º. The emerging light is always polarised in the orientation determined by the final filter it passes through. When the transmission axes are at 90°, the polarising filters are said to be crossed and no light passes through the filter B.

The diagram shows how the intensity of the emerging light from polariser B, varies as the transmission axis of polariser B, rotates.

Longitudinal waves cannot be polarised because the oscillations are always parallel to the direction of the energy transfer.
