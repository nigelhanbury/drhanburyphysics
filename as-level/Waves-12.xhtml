Interference

Interference is one of the main wave phenomena observed in everyday life. Interference occurs when more than one wave passes through each other at a specific point.

The example on the right, shows two pulses travelling towards each other on a rope. As can be seen, they pass through each other and emerge unchanged with their original wave profile intact. However, during the overlap where they pass through each other, the wave profile changes. This is where interference occurs. Notice that when the pulses completely overlap, they re-enforce each other or constructively interfere.

When waves pass through each other or overlap, we say they superpose, and this behaviour is governed by the principle of superposition.

The example on the right, shows two pulses travelling towards each other on a rope, but this time, one of the pulses is inverted. As can be seen, they pass through each other and emerge unchanged with their original wave profile intact as before.

However, this time the two pulses destructively interfere. In other words, they cancel each other out where they overlap (superpose). This is because the displacement of each particle on the rope for the two pulses are equal and opposite, and hence, their vector sum will be zero.

The Principal of Superpositioning

The principle of superpositioning states that if waves from two sources [or waves travelling by

different routes from the same source] occupy the same region, then the total displacement at any one point is the vector sum of their individual displacements at that point

More generally, we can say that interference occurs when two or more waves travelling in the same region superpose (overlap and interact).
