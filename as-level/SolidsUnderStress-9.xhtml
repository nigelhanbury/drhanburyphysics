Amorphous Materials

Materials that lack structure on a large scale are called amorphous. Typically, these are ceramic materials (clay, porcelain) and glass.

Diagram A on the right portrays silicon dioxide's crystalline structure (quartz) when cooled slowly, whereas diagram B depicts its amorphous structure (glass) when cooled rapidly.

Amorphous substances tend to be brittle, as they lack a crystalline structure and dislocations, which are essential for plastic deformation.

Brittle materials, including some crystalline materials (e.g., cast iron with numerous impurities and small grain size), exhibit perfect elasticity. Thus, amorphous materials follow Hooke's Law until reaching the breaking point.

Brittle Fracture due to Crack Propagation

Brittle materials are those that break without undergoing much plastic deformation. This means that once a brittle material reaches its yield stress, it will fracture almost immediately. One of the main reasons that brittle materials fracture is due to surface imperfections, such as cracks. When a crack forms on the surface of a brittle material, there are no intermolecular bonds in that region. This means that the internal bonds must resist any tension applied to the material, leading to an increase in tension in the inner bonds.

As the tension in the inner bonds increases, the bonds around the crack will eventually break. Once the bonds surrounding the crack break, the crack can propagate further into the material. The process then repeats, with even more tension in the inner bonds, causing additional bonds to break until the material ultimately fractures. This is why brittle materials are more susceptible to breaking at surface defects such as cracks.

Crack propagation occurs at the speed of sound in the material, resulting in catastrophic failure.
