Absorption Spectra

The main body of a star emits a continuous range of wavelengths, and hence, a continuous spectrum is produced. However, as the continuous spectrum of wavelengths passes through a star’s atmosphere, specific wavelengths are absorbed due to atomic excitation (More in unit2).

Black Body Radiation

Black body radiation is an important concept when investigating stars. A black body is a body (or surface) which absorbs all the electromagnetic radiation that falls upon it across all wavelengths. As such, any body that is classified as a black body will emit all wavelengths of the electromagnetic spectrum provided that the body is above absolute zero (0K). No body is a better emitter or absorber of radiation at any wavelength than a black body at the same temperature. Stars, such as our sun, are considered very good approximations of a black body. Not all wavelengths are emitted with the same intensity. The diagram on the right, shows the spectral intensity for each wavelength in the EM spectrum for bodies at different temperatures.

These curves are commonly referred to as black body curves. The visible spectrum has a range of 400nm to 700nm, and this is indicated on the graph. It is important to be able to sketch these curves at different temperatures and to use these curves to state the colour of a hot object, such as a star, at different temperatures. Notice, that as the temperature increases, the spectral peak increases its intensity, and its corresponding wavelength decreases or shifts to the left. The area under a black body curve is equal to the total power radiated by that black body.

In the diagram above, the object at 6000K will appear a blue white or blue. By inspection of the curve, we can see that all wavelengths that correspond to the visible spectrum are relatively intense, and hence, appears white, but since the dominant wavelength corresponds to the blue end of the spectrum, a blue appearance results. For the object at 5000K, the object will appear white, and the object at 4000K red.

Studies of the spectra presented here produced two empirical laws, which were later explained by the German Physicist Max Planck: Wien Displacement Law and Stefan-Boltzmann Law. These will be looked at later. A star emits strongly and equally in all directions, and as such, does not look black. However, it absorbs (almost) all radiation from other bodies that falls onto it, therefore, a star is almost a perfect black body.

Black body curves are given with temperatures in either degrees Kelvin or Celsius. The conversion is given by

T  K = T  C + 273°
