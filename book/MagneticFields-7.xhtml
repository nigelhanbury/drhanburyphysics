Magnetic Field Due to a Solenoid (coil)

When a current is passing through a coil of wire (solenoid), a magnetic field is generated around the solenoid, very much like that of a bar magnet. See diagram below.

The Right Hand Grip Rule can be used to predict the direction of the lines of flux. Grip the solenoid as shown above, with your fingers following the rotational sense of the current. Your thumb represents the direction of the field lines, running North to South. Knowing the direction of the field lines, the North and South poles are also known.

The magnetic flux density within a coil of length, L and comprising N turns of wire can be determined using B = μ  0 nI , where n represents the number of turns per unit length, given by n =  N _ L  .

Inside the solenoid, the lines of flux are parallel and equally spaced, indicating that the magnetic field strength is uniform everywhere inside the coil.

Iron core

The magnetic field of a coil is strengthened considerably if a core made from a ferromagnetic material (e.g. iron) is used. The core becomes magnetised and behaves like a permanent magnet, as long as the current is flowing. This arrangement is known as an Electromagnet. Ferromagnetic cores are used when strong magnetic fields are needed e.g. in transformers, relays, motors and generators. Iron can be easily magnetised and demagnetised, therefore, the electromagnet can be switched on and off. Permanent magnets can be made this way, by inserting a steel bar inside the solenoid. Steel can easily be magnetised but difficult to demagnetise. Once magnetised inside the solenoid, the magnetism persists after the current is switched off. Point of interest: The level of magnetisation in the steel is independent of the time that current is flowing, whether it is 0.1s or 5 hours!

Inserting an iron core into a solenoid will substantially increase the strength of the magnetic flux density.
