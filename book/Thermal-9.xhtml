The apparatus below, can be used to determine absolute zero. Previously, in section 3.3 ‘Kinetic Theory’, we looked at the Pressure Law. For a fixed mass of gas at constant volume, the pressure is directly proportional to the absolute temperature.

The water is heated and the temperature and pressure are recorded at intervals. Here, the thermometer displays the temperature in Celsius. The pressure P, and temperature θ, are plotted as shown. The graph above, represents the results of the experiment taken at 2 different fixed volumes. Once the points are plotted, a line of best fit must be drawn. This line is then extended back until it intercepts the temperature axis. The intercept occurs at -273°C. The intercept point is independent of the fixed volume chosen, as can be seen from the graph. This temperature is known as absolute zero. This is what defines the Kelvin scale. In other words, 0 Kelvin equal -273°C.

On inspection of the graph, as the temperature decreases, the pressure decreases. This makes sense, as there will be a decrease in the root mean square speed of the gas molecules, and hence, a decrease in the frequency of collisions between the molecules and the container walls. The internal energy of the gas will decrease as the temperature continues to fall, as the mean kinetic energy of the molecules decreases. At absolute zero, the pressure is zero, indicating no molecular motion and zero internal energy. In fact, owing to quantum effects, the internal energy can never become zero, instead, reaching a ground state. Therefore, absolute zero occurs when the internal energy of the gas has reached a minimum.
