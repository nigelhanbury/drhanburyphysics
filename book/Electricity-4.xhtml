How Current Depends on Drift Velocity of Free Electrons in a Metal

Within a conductor (metal), not all the orbital electrons are bound to the nuclei. A small proportion will be free to move within the conductor forming what is known as a sea of free electrons. This leaves the metal atoms with a net positive charge, and hence, are known as positive ions (or just metal ions). As the metal will have a temperature, these metal ions will oscillate (vibrate) about fixed positions within a regular crystal lattice. These free electrons contribute to the random thermal energy of the metal as they frequently collide with the metal ions, causing the free electrons to change direction. Since there are a large number of free electrons moving continuously in random directions, there is no net flow of charge in any one direction. Typical speeds for these free electrons are of the order 106 ms-1. Free electrons behave as an ideal gas.

When a potential difference (or p.d.) is applied across a conductor, each free electron will experience a force, which will cause them to travel in one direction with a mean drift velocity. On average, the free electrons move slowly in one direction through the metal lattice when there is a p.d. across the conductor because they collide constantly with the lattice ions. The term drift velocity is described as the average displacement of the free electrons along the conductor per unit time.
