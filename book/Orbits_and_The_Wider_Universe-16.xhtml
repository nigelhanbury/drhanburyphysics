Since Hubble’s law tells us that all the distant galaxies are receding away from us, they must have been closer together in the past, and at some point, occupying a single point. At this point, we can consider it to be the beginning of the Universe. With this in mind, we can estimate how long ago all the galaxies occupied the same point and this will give us the age of the Universe. In order to do this, we must assume that each galaxy has been receding away from us at a constant speed.

We can make use of the simple formula, time =  distance _ velocity  .

It is important to make the point, that the galaxy in the above diagram with a recessional velocity, v, has always been traveliing with this velocity and always will.In other words, whatever the recessional velocity of a particular galaxy is, it is constant and not change over time.

Making use of Hubble’s law, v = H  0 D , we can write t =  D _ v  , which results in t =  D __  H  0 D  .

This simplifies to t =  1 __  H  0   . The age of the Universe is simply the reciprocal of the Hubble constant.

This is only an approximation for the following reasons:

That the galaxy has been travelling at the same constant speed throughout its existence. This is not true in practice, since it must have gained some gravitational potential energy, and hence, lost some of its initial kinetic energy. This means that the galaxy’s present speed is less than its mean speed, and if the latter had been used in the calculation, a lower value would have been obtained for the age of the Universe.

There is an uncertainty in the value of H0 and this means that the value obtained for the age is also uncertain.

The galaxies formed some considerable time after the Big Bang, and this time delay has been ignored in the calculation.

For all the above reasons, it is preferable to say that:

Age of the Universe, t ≈  1 __  H  0  

This age is sometimes referred to as the Hubble time. Hubble time is defined as the reciprocal of the Hubble constant.
