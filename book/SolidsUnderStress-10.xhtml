Pre-Stressed Concrete

Concrete is a brittle material used widely in the building industry.

The concrete depicted on the right is not pre-stressed and exhibits tension on the lower side, resulting in fracture points due to surface imperfections.

However, if concrete remains under compression, there is no risk of it breaking. To achieve this, it is necessary to pre-stress the concrete using tendons made of steel bars.

The process of pre-stressing concrete involves the use of tendons, which are typically steel bars that are placed under tension within a mould before the concrete is poured in. The concrete is then poured into the mould over the pre-stressed tendons. Once the concrete has solidified and is strong enough to hold its shape, the tendons are released from their tension. As they relax, they pull the concrete towards them, creating a compressive force within the concrete. This compressive force helps to counteract the tension that the concrete will experience when subjected to external loads or forces, thus increasing its strength and durability. By pre-stressing concrete in this way, it becomes capable of bearing heavier loads and can withstand harsher environments than non-pre-stressed concrete.

Another method to increase the breaking stress of brittle materials is to produce fibres. A few molecules across, fibres cannot have surface imperfections and as such, crack propagation does not happen.

Polymeric Materials

Polymers are a group of materials that include rubber, polythene, melamine, and nylon, among others. They are composed of long chain molecules that consist of repeating structural units known as monomers.

The diagram on the right shows a generalized polymer, which is not representative of any specific compound. The polymer is composed of monomers that are connected together to form a long chain molecule. These monomers are linked by carbon bonds, which allow the polymer molecule to take on a wide range of different shapes due to the rotation of the C-C bond.

Additionally, the diagram on the right shows a polymer chain molecule that is tangled and contains cross-links. These cross-links create a network structure that gives the polymer its unique properties. While the specific properties of a polymer depend on its chemical composition and structure, all polymers share the common characteristic of having long chain molecules made up of repeating monomer units.
