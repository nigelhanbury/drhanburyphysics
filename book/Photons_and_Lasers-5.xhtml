Characteristics of a Photocell

As stated above, if radiation of frequency greater than the threshold frequency is incident upon the photocell, a current will be measured on the micro ammeter. The graph on the right, shows how the current measured increases for increasing light intensity. We can see that the current is directly proportional to the incident light intensity, provided the area of illumination is constant.

Remembering that intensity is defined as the energy per unit time per unit area or power per unit area, we can express the power in terms of photons, P=nhf, where n, represents the number of photons per unit time incident on the photocell cathode, and the photon energy, hf, again assuming the area of illumination is constant.

By keeping the frequency constant, and hence, the photon energy, and doubling the intensity, the number of photons striking the cathode per unit time, n, doubles. As there is a one to one interaction with the surface electrons, the number of photoelectrons emitted per unit time doubles. Therefore, the number of electrons passing a point in one second doubles, and hence, the current doubles.

Increasing the intensity at constant frequency does not change the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons, as this depends purely on the frequency of the incident radiation. Remember that

Increasing the intensity results in an increase in the number of photoelectrons emitted per unit time only.

If we replace light with that of a higher frequency and keep the same intensity, then the number of photons incident on the photocell per unit time will decrease as each photon will carry a larger energy since . This results in fewer photoelectrons per unit time being emitted. Even though there are fewer photoelectrons emitted per unit time, the maximum kinetic energy will have increased since each photon that is absorbed by a surface electron carries more energy.
