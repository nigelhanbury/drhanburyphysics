Polarisation

Before we discuss polarisation, it is a good idea to remind ourselves exactly what is meant by a transverse wave. A transverse wave is one where the particle oscillations are at right angles to the direction of travel of the wave. The diagram on the right, represents two transverse waves, where the particle oscillations are perpendicular to each other. As can be seen, these oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of wave travel.

When discussing polarisation, it is in the context of electromagnetic waves (EM Waves). As a reminder, the EM spectrum is made up of a continuous range of wavelengths broken down into sections known as: radio waves, microwaves, infra red, visible light, ultra violet, x-rays and gamma rays. Polarisation is mainly concerned with radio waves and visible light. EM waves are transverse waves, but there are no particle oscillations as these waves are a result of disturbances within electric and magnetic fields. Electric and magnetic fields will be fully covered in A2 units 4.2 and 4.4 respectively. Therefore, EM waves do not need a medium to travel through, and so, can travel through a vacuum at the speed of light. Here, we are only interested in the electric field disturbances as it is the electric field that mostly interacts with matter, such as your retina, enabling you to see and detect colour etc.

Definition of a Polarised Wave

A polarised wave is a transverse wave in which particle oscillations occur in only one of the directions at right angles to the direction of wave propagation. Note, that for polarised EM waves, the direction of the oscillating electric fields occur in only one of the directions at right angles to the direction of wave propagation.

Polarisers

The diagram on the right, represents a polarised EM wave passing through a polariser at three different settings. A polariser contains a transmission axis, which only allows oscillations parallel to the transmission axis through.

In the first diagram, the direction of oscillation of the electric field of the polarised wave is parallel to the transmission axis, and so, no attenuation occurs, and 100% transmission is obtained.

In the second diagram, the direction of oscillation of the polarised wave is at 45º to the transmission axis. In this situation, only the component of the oscillating electric field parallel to the transmission axis can pass through, and so, attenuation occurs.

In the third diagram, the direction of oscillation of the polarised wave is perpendicular to the transmission axis, and so, 0% transmission is obtained.
