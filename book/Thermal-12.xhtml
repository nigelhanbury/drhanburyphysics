Initially, the power delivered by the heating element (immersion heater), needs to be established. This is done by measuring the potential difference across the heater by connecting a voltmeter across it and measuring the current through the heater by connecting an ammeter in series. Then, the power can be calculated using p = VI .

The mass of the metal block can be measured using a digital balance.

It is important to place insulation around the metal block, in an attempt to reduce heat loss to the surroundings.

A drop of oil is placed in the drilled out hole where the thermometer is inserted, as to ensure good thermal contact is maintained.

Note the initial temperature displayed on the thermometer, and then record the temperature and time at regular intervals, such as every 30 seconds. Once complete, a graph of Δθ against time can be plotted. Here, Δθ represents the increase in temperature relative to the initial temperature.

Using Q = mcΔθ , where the heat delivered to the metal block Q, is given by, Q = pΔt = VIΔt , leads to VIΔt = mcΔθ .

After re-arranging, c = (  VI _ m  ) (  Δt _ Δθ  ) . On inspection of the above graph, and noting that the gradient is equal to  Δθ _ Δt  , we can write c =  VI _ m × gradient  .

If the metal block is not well insulated, then a linear graph will not be achieved. On inspection of the graph to the left, the initial portion appears approximately linear. Early on in the experiment, the temperature difference between the block and the surroundings is small, therefore, the rate of heat loss to the environment is small.

When the temperature difference between the block and the environment is much larger, there is a much larger rate of heat loss. Therefore, over a fixed interval of time, a smaller increase in temperature Δθ , is achieved for the same quantity of supplied heat.

Eventually, the rate at which heat is supplied to the block will equal the rate at which heat is lost to the surroundings. At this point, the temperature will become constant, and the block will have reached thermal equilibrium.

As it will now require a greater amount of heat to raise the temperature of the solid by 1°C, the measured specific heat capacity will be an overestimate.
