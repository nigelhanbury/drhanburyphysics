The Pressure Law

The apparatus to the right, demonstrates how the temperature of an ideal gas can be increased while keeping the volume constant.

Here, dry air is a good approximation to an ideal gas. The tubing is assumed to have negligible volume compared to the flask.

Heat is applied to the water bath to increase the temperature of the water and the dry air. It is assumed that the thermometer reading represents the temperature of the dry air. In order for this to be a valid assumption, time must be provided between each reading to allow the dry air and water to reach thermal equilibrium and share the same temperature. The pressure can be read off the pressure gauge once the reading is stable.

P = (  nR _ V  ) T

The graph above, shows that the pressure is directly proportional to the Kelvin temperature, P ∝ T . Remember, that T is measured in Kelvin. The equation that describes the above graph is given by P = (  nR _ V  ) T . From the equation, it can be seen that the gradient is equal to  nR _ V  . Increasing the number of moles n, or decreasing the volume V, will increase the gradient.

Since  P _ T  = constant , we can write

  P  1  __  T  1   =   P  2  __  T  2     

Example Problem

The pressure of the air in the tyres of a car before a journey is 2.2 × 10  5 Pa at 18°C. After travelling some distance, the temperature of the air in the tyres rises to 54°C. Calculate the new pressure of the air. Assume the volume of air in the tyre remains constant.

Solution

Using   P  1  __  T  1   =   P  2  __  T  2   , we can write  2.2 ×  10  5  _______  (18 + 273.16)   =   P  2  __  (54 + 273.16)   ⇒ P  2 = 2.5 × 10  5 Pa
