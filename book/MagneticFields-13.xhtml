Particle Accelerators

Particle accelerators are used to investigate the structure of sub-atomic particles. High speed particles are accelerated using electric and sometimes magnetic fields and are ‘fired’ at other particles to create other particles during the collision. By analysing the properties of the new particles created, a greater understanding of the underlying physics may be determined.

The Linear Accelerator [LINAC]

An alternating potential difference is applied across the drift tubes as shown in the diagram. This produces an alternating electric field across the gaps between these tubes. The charged particles are accelerated by the electric field.

The particle travels in drift tubes between 'kicks' by the electric field produced by the alternating pd. As the particle leaves a drift tube, there must be an accelerating field across the gap, so the tube the charged particle is within, must be of the same potential as the particle's charge.

This ensures that the particle is repelled away from the tube it has just left and attracted to the tube it is about to enter, as the potential of the next tube is opposite, as shown in the diagram. When the particle enters the drift tube it experiences no electric field, and therefore, it 'drifts' at the same velocity until it reaches the end of the tube. By the time the particle exits the tube, the polarity of all tubes are reversed, and the process repeats. This ensures that the particle is accelerated across all the gaps.

In the example to the right, a proton is accelerated towards tube B, as tubes A and B have a positive and negative potential respectively. When the proton reaches the mid - point of tube B, the polarity is reversed. This ensures that the proton is then accelerated across the next gap as tubes B and C will have a positive and negative potential respectively. This process repeats as the polarity of all the tubes will again, reverse every half cycle when the proton reaches the mid - point of the next tube.

The lengths of the tubes are synchronised with the alternating potential difference, so that the proton spends an equal time of half a cycle in each tube. The increase in length reflects the fact that the proton’s velocity is increasing.

In reality, for a single AC source, the gap between drift tubes must increase, so that the proton arrives at the gap at the correct point within the cycle.
