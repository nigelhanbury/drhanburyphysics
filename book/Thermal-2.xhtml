The internal energy of a system U, (collection of atoms/molecules) is defined as the sum of the random distribution of kinetic and potential energies of those atoms/molecules.

The internal energy of a system, therefore, depends on its temperature. We will discuss how the internal energy of a system changes as the temperature of a solid is raised, through to its gas state.

Consider thermal energy is being supplied to a solid at a constant rate. The diagram on the right, represents how the temperature will change within that solid, and correspondingly, through each of the other states.

During each phase change (melting and boiling), the temperature is constant.

Time taken to change all of the liquid to a gas is much longer than from a solid to a liquid.

When a solid is heated, the molecules will oscillate with greater amplitude and frequency, increasing the translational kinetic energy of the molecules. There will be a slight increase in the mean separation between the molecules, and therefore, a slight increase in the potential energy (negligible) will result. Since the translational kinetic energy increases greatly, and potential energy increases a little, the internal energy U, increases.

During the melting phase, all the energy supplied is used to break some of the bonds between the molecules, and therefore, increasing their potential energy. There is no increase in the translational kinetic energy, and the temperature remains constant. The temperature remains constant until all of the solid has changed to liquid. This supplied energy is known as latent heat. The internal energy U, increases.

Once all the solid has melted, and if further thermal energy is supplied, the temperature will begin to rise. The molecules of the liquid gain translational kinetic energy (they are moving but not oscillating). Their potential energy only increases slightly (negligible) as their mean separation increases slightly. The internal energy U, increases. The molecules continually break and remake bonds and are able to move around each other.

If the liquid continues to be heated, a temperature will be reached when the molecules have enough translational kinetic energy to break all their bonds completely, and escape from the surface of the liquid. This is the boiling point of the liquid. The thermal energy supplied is used to break the bonds, and not increase the translational kinetic energy of the molecules. The temperature remains constant. The potential energy of the molecules increases to zero. The internal energy again increases.

A larger amount of time is spent during the boiling phase, as more thermal energy is required. This is because all of the bonds need to be broken.
