1.5 Solids Under Stress

Hooke’s Law

Experimental Procedure

Set up the apparatus as shown, but with no load on the spring.

With the aid of the set square (to avoid parallax error), note the position of the bottom of the lower loop of the spring.

Hang a mass of 100g from the lower end of the spring. Note the new reading for the position of the bottom of the spring.

Increase the load in 100g steps, up to a maximum of 800g, noting the position of the bottom of the spring each time.

Decrease the load in 100g steps, noting the position of the bottom of the spring each time.

Calculate the mean extension for each load.

Plot a force-extension graph.

The force required to extend the spring is commonly referred to as the load. The diagram on the right, represents a typical load-extension graph. Up to the limit of proportionality, the load, F is directly proportional to the extension, x. In other words, F ∝ x .

Beyond the elastic limit, the spring will not return to its original length (plastic deformation). These two points are generally at different locations, though WJEC treat them as the same point.

F ∝ x can be replaced with F = kx , where k is known as the spring constant.

The spring constant is the force per unit extension of the spring and has units: N m  -1 , where k is also referred to as the spring stiffness.

Definition of Hooke’s Law

The tension in a spring or wire is proportional to its extension from its natural length, provided the extension is not too great.

Definition of Spring Constant

The spring constant, k is the force per unit extension.

Unit: N m  -1
