Unit 3, Section 5: Nuclear Decay

Beta Decay

As discussed in the AS physics course, there are 2 types of beta decay, though this would have been discussed under the context of electron or positron emission. These are beta+ and beta-, commonly written as 𝛽  + and 𝛽  − respectively. This type of decay was explored in detail within ‘Particle Physics’.

When a nucleus undergoes 𝛽  − decay, a neutron within the nucleus transforms into a proton, along with an electron and anti-electron neutrino. The electron is then ejected at approximately 95% the speed of light. This high speed electron is known as a beta particle. The charge on the beta particle is –e. The nuclear decay equation can be written as  Z  A  X → Z + 1  A  Y +  − 1  0  𝛽  +  _ ν  e .

When a nucleus undergoes 𝛽  + decay, a proton within the nucleus transforms into a neutron, along with a positron and electron neutrino. The nuclear decay equation can be written as  Z  A  X → Z − 1  A  Y +  + 1  0  𝛽  +  𝜈  e .

Since beta particles carry a smaller charge compared to alpha, and travel at a much greater speed, the probability of causing ionisation is smaller, and so, is deemed less ionising than alpha. As a consequence, beta radiation is absorbed by several mm of Aluminium, and has a range of approximately 1m in air.

Gamma Decay

When a nucleus undergoes either an alpha or beta decay, the nucleus is often left in an excited state. The nucleus is a bound system, and as such, will contain discrete energy levels. When the energy of the nucleus drops to a lower energy level, a gamma photon is released. Note, that the energy gap between nuclear energy levels is much larger than that of atomic energy levels, and so, photons of much higher frequency are emitted.

Since a gamma photon carries zero charge, it has the least ionising effect. As a consequence, gamma radiation is absorbed by several cm of Lead, and has a range of hundreds of metres in air.

Since there is no structural change to the nucleus following gamma emission, the element remains unchanged.

𝛽  − nuclear decay equation

 Z  A  X → Z + 1  A  Y +  − 1  0  𝛽  +  _ ν  e

𝛽  + nuclear decay equation

 Z  A  X → Z − 1  A  Y +  + 1  0  𝛽  +  𝜈  e

Since there is no structural change to the nucleus following gamma emission, the element remains unchanged.
