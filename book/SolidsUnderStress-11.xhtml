Stress-Strain Graph for Rubber

The stress-strain diagram on the right shows the behaviour of rubber and is highly non-linear. The top curve represents the loading phase, during which stress is applied to the material. The second curve represents unloading, as stress is removed. Because the strain returns to zero, the rubber is behaving elastically.

The area under the loading curve represents the work done in stretching the rubber and is stored as elastic potential energy. The area under the unloading curve, on the other hand, represents the energy released by the rubber. The difference between the two areas is given by the shaded region. This shaded area represents the amount of energy that is converted into thermal energy within the rubber as the strain returns to zero.

When a polymeric material, like rubber is under tension, the force required is much less compared to stretching a crystalline or amorphous material as the bonds are being untangled and not stretched. The total extension is only limited by cross-linkages between molecules or the level of entanglement.

Region A:

At low strains, the rubber displays stiffness due to the stress required to break the Van der Waals bonding between cross-links.

Region B:

This is less stiff than Section A, as small stresses can produce large strains. In this case, stress is needed to untangle the chain molecules (bonds), and the resistance to extension is due to thermal opposition - the thermal motion of atoms within the molecules.

Region C:

This section is significantly stiffer than the previous two sections, as the chain molecules are fully straightened. The stress needed in this case is to increase the intermolecular distances.

When the tension is removed:

A new random arrangement of molecules takes place.

The rubber contracts and regains its original length.

Some energy is converted by intermolecular collisions into random kinetic energy of the

Molecules (thermal energy stored in the material).

The work done in contraction is less than the work done in extension. This is called hysteresis (difference in areas between loading and unloading stress-strain graph).
