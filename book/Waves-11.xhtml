2.5 Wave Properties

Diffraction

Diagram 1 below, represents plane wavefronts passing through a gap (aperture), where the gap width is much greater than the incident wavelength. As can be seen, the wavefronts emerge unchanged. Diagram 2 represents plane wavefronts passing through a gap, where the gap width is much smaller than previously, and the wavefronts spread out into the geometric shadow. Diagram 3 shows the effect where the width of the gap is equal to (or less than) the incident wavelength. Here, the diffracted wavefronts spread out forming semi circular paths. The gap behaves as though it is a point source of waves. Note, the wavelength of the diffracted wavefronts is equal to the wavelength of the incident wavefronts.

When wavefronts pass through a gap or encounter an obstacle, the wavefronts spread out. This is known as diffraction.

The diagram on the right, represents plane wavefronts encountering an obstacle. As can be seen, the wavefronts are diffracted around the obstacle into the geometric shadow. The greater the wavelength of the incident wavefronts, the greater the diffraction effect.

The phenomena of diffraction occurs for both transverse and longitudinal waves, such as EM waves and sound.

Diffraction of light cannot be noticed in everyday life as the wavelength of visible light is much smaller than the objects around us and also much smaller than the door frames within buildings.

However, we can notice the diffraction of sound waves entering a room through an open door. Since the wavelength of sound waves are approximately equal to the width of a door frame, the sound waves diffract and spread out into the room, allowing people in the far corner of a room to hear sounds outside.
