Digital Transmission

A multimode fibre has a relatively large diameter core around 50-100µm. In a multimode fibre, there are multiple paths which the light can follow from one end of the fibre to the other following a continues range of zig-zag routes. This causes dispersion. Data is sent down an optical fibre as a series of light (infrared) pulses as depicted in diagram A. These pulses represent the ‘0’ and ‘1’ of binary data representation, i.e, ‘1’ = pulse and ‘0’ = no pulse. As each pulse travels down the fibre, some of the light takes the shortest path and other light takes longer paths. This causes the pulse to spread out, and therefore, consecutive pulses become closer together on exit or even begin to overlap as shown in diagram B.

When the pulses overlap it becomes impossible to decode the data (0’s cannot be distinguished from 1’s). This broadening or dispersion, therefore, limits the rate at which pulses can be transmitted, i.e. it limits the bit-rate, and therefore, the rate at which data can be transferred. Multimode fibres are used where a high bit-rate (number of 0’s and 1’s per second) is not necessary or where the data is transferred over only short distances, such as within a building.

Monomode Optical Fibres

In a monomode fibre the core is very thin. The diameter is just a few wavelengths of infrared radiation. As a consequence, there is effectively only one path or mode for the light to follow. All of the light travels parallel to the axis of the core and so there is no spreading or dispersion of the light pulses. Therefore, the data can be transferred at a higher rate, and over greater distances (e.g. between continents) compared to a multimode fibre.
