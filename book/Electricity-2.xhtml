Charging by Friction

It is important to understand the mechanism that results in materials becoming charged via friction. Consider the diagram on the right that represents a perspex rod acquiring a positive charge after being rubbed with a dry silk cloth. When the two materials are brought into contact, one or more orbital electrons are transferred from some of the atoms that make up the rod to some of the atoms that make up the cloth. This results in some of the atoms that make up the dry cloth containing more orbital electrons than there are protons in the nucleus. This results in the dry cloth containing a surplus of negatively charged electrons, and so, acquiring a net negative charge. It is important to note, that when materials become charged, it is only the negatively charged electrons that are mobile. Positive charge does not get transferred as the nuclei of atoms are in fixed positions. Positive charge is the result of a deficit of electrons.

The Van de Graaff Generator

The Van de Graaff generator is a useful tool in school laboratories to demonstrate static electricity. In short, an electric motor drives a rubber belt, which charges a metal sphere (dome). As the belt continuously moves, electrons are transferred from the metal dome to the belt leaving a deficit of electrons, and hence, a net positive charge on the dome.

If a conductor is placed close to the dome as shown in the diagram, a potential difference (voltage) will develop across the air gap. As the charge on the dome increases, the potential difference across the air gap increases until the air gap become ionised. At this point, the air gap becomes conducting, allowing electrons to flow from the metal to the dome. An electrical discharge results.

It is only the negatively charged electrons that are mobile. Positive charge does not get transferred as the nuclei of atoms are in fixed positions.

The SI unit of charge is the Coulomb, symbol C. Charge is quantized. This means that the net charge on a particle, or an object, is a multiple of the elementary charge, e, which is 1.60 × 10-19 Coulombs (i.e. only a small fraction of 1 Coulomb).

An electron carries a charge of -1.60 x 10-19 C.

A proton carries a charge of +1.60 x 10-19 C.
