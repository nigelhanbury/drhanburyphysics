2.3 Direct Current Circuits

Current and Potential Difference in Series and Parallel Circuits

So far we have only dealt with very simple circuits made up of at most two resistors. In this section, we will examine more complex circuits involving multiple components connected in series and parallel. Firstly, we will focus on electric current within these circuits followed by potential difference.

Electric Current

Circuit 1 represents a series circuit. A series circuit can be easily recognised as components are directly connected together forming a single loop.

In a series circuit, the current is the same everywhere. In the case of circuit 1, all the ammeters would display the same reading as all points within that circuit will have the same current.

This can be related to the conservation of charge. Consider point, X, within the circuit shown. The quantity of charge approaching that point in one second (blue) must equal the quantity of charge leaving that point in one second (purple). Therefore, the current flowing into the component shown must equal the current flowing through that component.

Circuit 2 represents a parallel circuit. A parallel circuit can be easily recognised as the circuit is made up of more than one loop. Again, using the conservation of charge, the quantity of charge approaching a junction in one second (blue) must equal the total quantity of charge leaving that junction in one second (purple). Therefore, I=I1+I2. For this particular example, we can see that there is a greater quantity of charge branching to the right in one second compared to that branching down in one second, therefore, I2>I1.

This can be generalised into a single statement, known as Kirchhoff’s First Law. The sum of the currents entering a junction must equal the sum of the currents leaving that junction. This is a statement of the conservation of charge and is Universal. Note, not a WJEC requirement.
