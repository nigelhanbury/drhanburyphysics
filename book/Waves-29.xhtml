Refraction of White Light

Different frequencies of EM waves have different refractive indices. Each of the frequencies (colours) that make up white light have their own refractive index, and therefore, will refract by differing amounts when passing from one medium to another. This is called dispersion.

Diagram B, represents a red and violet ray incident on a prism. Violet light has a higher refractive index than red, and therefore, will refract the greatest when passing across the air/glass boundary. Diagram B, represents white light entering the same prism at the same angle. As can be seen, each of the frequencies or colours that make up white light are refracted by differing amounts, and therefore, the full spectrum of visible light is observed.

Measuring Refractive Index

The arrangement should be set up as shown in the diagram.

The angle of refraction, θr, can be measured by drawing in the line joining the incident and emergent rays for different values of the angle of incidence, θi.

The angles can be measured using a protractor, after drawing in the normal.

Using Snell’s Law, we can write nasinθi=ngsinθr. Since na≈1, this reduces to sinθi=ngsinθr.

A graph can be plotted of Sinθi against sinθr. The refractive index, ng, can then be determined by measuring the gradient.

Reflection

The diagram on the right, represents a light ray incident on a boundary for increasing angle of incidence. As can be seen, every time a light ray is refracted, a partially reflected ray is also produced. The greater the angle of incidence, the greater the intensity of the reflected ray. Remember, that angle of reflection equals angle of incidence. The reflected light is also partially plane polarised.
