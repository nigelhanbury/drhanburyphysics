3.4 Thermal Physics

Thermal physics is mainly concerned with the macroscopic world, in terms of changing pressure, volume and temperature, and how energy can be extracted from such processes. One example of this, is the combustion engine. Before we move onto this topic, it is important to remind ourselves of the structure of solids, liquids and gases.

Within a solid, each molecule is vibrating about a fixed position. The molecules are close together and arranged in a regular pattern for many hundreds of layers. There are strong intermolecular bonds, which hold the molecules in their regular pattern. The molecules have both kinetic energy and potential energy. The reason they have potential energy is owing to the forces acting between them.

Solids

Liquids

Within a liquid, the bonds between the molecules are continuously breaking and reforming, allowing them to move around each other. As a consequence, the mean intermolecular separation is slightly larger than that of a solid. The potential energy of the molecules is greater than that of a solid as work has to be done in order to increase the distance between them.

Gases

Within a gas, the molecules are considered so far apart, that it is assumed that they experience no intermolecular forces. The mean separation between the molecules is very large compared to solids and liquids. As there are no intermolecular forces, the molecules are considered to have zero potential energy.

Note, this can be a point of confusion. Work has to be done on the molecules to increase their mean separation.

This work is transformed into the stored potential energy of the molecules, and so, the potential energy must increase for increasing separation.

It is usual to define zero potential energy when the separation between the molecules is infinite. Therefore, the potential energy has a negative value and increases up to zero.

In the gas state, the molecules are considered effectively infinitely far apart and have zero potential energy.
