Atomic Energy Levels and Spectra

When the light emitted from flames, hot gases, or gases in discharge tubes at low pressures, is passed through a diffraction grating, a series of coloured lines is produced on a dark background.

This is called a line spectrum, and the diagram above, represents a line (or emission) spectrum produced by Hydrogen. Each colour relates to light of a specific wavelength. Line spectra are only produced by unattached or isolated atoms, such as those in hot gases. When the atoms are closely packed, such as in solids, a continuous spectrum is produced. The presence of only certain lines in atomic spectra led Niels Bohr (1915) to conclude that electrons orbit in shells around the nucleus of an atom, and that the shells have discrete energy levels. In the macroscopic world, we are used to dealing with objects or systems that possess kinetic and potential energy, and that these energies can take on any value. In the atomic world, quantum mechanics takes over. Here, atoms have energy, but they cannot take on any value, only discrete values. The energy of an atom can increase or decrease, but only in discrete quantities. Therefore, we say that the energy is quantised.

The atoms of a given element have a characteristic set of energy levels. The diagram on the right, represents Hydrogen. An electron is only permitted to move from one level to another by either gaining or losing a specific amount of energy. In an isolated (unattached) atom, an electron is not allowed to have an amount of energy which would place it between two permitted levels.

In the diagram above, each energy level is characterised by its principal quantum number, n. Here, we have shown the first six energy levels of Hydrogen. It is important to note, that the energy levels have negative values. This is because when the electron is infinitely far away from the nucleus, its energy is defined to be zero, therefore, as the electron moves further from the nucleus, its energy increases up to zero. The energy of a Hydrogen atom is calculated by adding together the electrons kinetic and electrostatic potential energy. The lowest energy level is known as the ground state.
