The mean power taken from the supply by the capacitor is zero.

The graphs on the right should help clarify. We know that the power at any instant being transferred to the capacitor is given by P = IV. By inspecting the graphs, we can see that there is a phase difference of  π _ 2  radians between the voltage V, and current I.

Let us look at the first quarter of a cycle, which is represented by the shaded area A. We can see that both V and I are positive, and so, the power must be positive and transferred to the capacitor. In the next quarter cycle, represented by the shaded area B, V is positive and I is negative. This results in a negative power, which leads to power being transferred from the capacitor back to the supply.

Therefore, during each half cycle, and hence, a full cycle, there is no net power transfer to the capacitor. We know from unit 4.1 ‘Capacitors’, that energy is stored in a charged capacitor. Since Q ∝ V, part B of the graph represents the capacitor discharging, and hence, the capacitor is transferring its stored energy back to the supply.

The mean power transferred to a capacitor over one cycle is zero.

Phasor diagrams are an extremely useful tool in solving AC problems, particularly at A level. The diagram on the right, represents a rotating vector of length V0, rotating with an angular frequency of ω. This is a geometric representation of a sinusoidal function, such as the alternating voltage shown. The length of the vector represents the peak voltage V0, and the vertical projection, V0sin(ωt) , represents the voltage at that instant at time t.
