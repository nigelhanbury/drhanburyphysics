Consider an alternating supply connected across a capacitor as shown in the diagram below. The instantaneous voltage V, across the capacitor is shown. Referring back to unit 4.1 ‘Capacitors’, we know that the charge Q, stored on the capacitor plates is given by Q = CV, where C, represents the capacitance of the capacitor. Since V = V  0 sin (ωt) , we can write Q = CV  0 sin (ωt) , which shows that the charge varies sinusoidally with time, as shown below.

As can be seen from both the equation and graph, the charge stored oscillates between positive and negative values. This can cause confusion as it implies that the charge is positive for one half of the cycle, and negative for the other. This is not the case, as the capacitor is simply reversing its polarity every half cycle. In other words, plate A, is positively charged and plate B, negatively charged for one half of the cycle, and for the next half cycle, plate B, is positively charge and plate A, negatively charged.

The current I, at any instant, is given by the gradient of the tangent, or I =  dQ _ dt  =  d _ dt  (C V  0 sin (ωt) ) .

This results in I = ωC V  0 cos (ωt) . This can be written in the form I = I  0 cos (ωt) , where I  0 = ωC V  0 .

By direct comparison with V = V  0 sin (ωt) , it can be seen that the current leads the voltage by  π _ 2  radians.

Phase Difference Between Current and Voltage

For a capacitor, I leads V by  π _ 2  radians or 90°.
