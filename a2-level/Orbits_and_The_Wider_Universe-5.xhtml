Binary Systems

A binary system is a system of two masses orbiting their common centre of mass (barycentre). Many star systems in our Milkyway galaxy are binary systems. Planetary orbits are treated as binary as the planet and star orbit their common centre of mass.

The example to the right consists of two stars of mass m1 and m2. Both objects orbit about their common centre of mass with the same angular velocity, and therefore, the period of each star must be the same.

Determination of the centre of mass

The centre of mass (COM) of the two masses, m1 and m2 is that point where all the mass appears to act.

In other words, if there was an imaginary massless rod joining the two masses, then the system would balance if pivoted at the COM.

Applying the principle of moments, m  1 r  1 = m  2 r  2 and the distance between the masses given by d = r  1 + r  2 , produces the following:

By inspecting both formulae, r  2 ≈ d if m1 is much greater than m2 ( m1 >> m2 ).
