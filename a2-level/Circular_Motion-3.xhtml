Diagram A, on the right represents the change in velocity Δv, as depicted previously. The angular displacement θ, is equal to θ = ωt . Notice that the arc length is shown, where this represents the arc length of a circle of radius v. Since the particle is moving with constant speed, the length of the velocity vectors are equal.

Diagram B, represents the change in velocity over a very small time interval Δt , and hence, the angular displacement Δθ , is small. In this case, the arc length can be considered the magnitude of Δv.

Therefore, Δv ≈ vΔθ and Δv ≈ vωΔt , where Δθ = ωΔt .

We can now state the approximate acceleration a, as a ≈  Δv _ Δt  , and so, a ≈ vω .

In the limit as Δt → 0 ,  Δv _ Δt  →  dv _ dt  , and so, the instantaneous acceleration is given by a = vω .

Substituting v = rω into a = vω gives a = r ω  2 .

On inspection of diagram B, we can see that as Δθ → 0 , the change in velocity, Δv becomes perpendicular to v. Therefore, the instantaneous acceleration acts towards the centre of rotation.

Centripetal Force

Since any particle travelling with uniform circular motion, experiences an acceleration towards the centre of the circle, a resultant force must be acting on that particle towards the centre of the circle.

This is an important point to note, that owing to F = ma , the resultant force acting on any mass, must act in the exact same direction as the acceleration, as they are both vector quantities.

This resultant force towards the centre of the circle is known as the centripetal force.

Using F = ma , the centripetal force F, can be expressed as: F = mr ω  2 or F =  m v  2  ___ r  .
