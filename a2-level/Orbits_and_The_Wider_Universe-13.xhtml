Dark Matter and The Motion of Objects in Galaxies

A galaxy is a gravitationally bound system of stars, and each galaxy, depending on its size, consists of 109 to 1014 stars rotating slowly around its centre of mass. Galaxies usually have a region where the concentration of stars is greatest and this is called the nucleus.

All the stars in the galaxy rotate around this central bulge. The rotational period of each star depends on its distance from the nucleus. The further out a star is, the longer it takes to complete one rotation.

Our own galaxy, known as the Milky Way, is a spiral galaxy having a mean diameter of about 100,000 light-years. This means that a beam of light would take 100,000 years to cross the diameter of the Milky Way.

The Sun, one of the billions of stars in the Milky Way, is located at a point about 28,000 light years from the galactic centre and it takes about 230 million years to complete one revolution.

Dark matter is the term used to describe the discrepancy between the observed mass in the universe and the theoretical mass measured by observing the behaviour of galaxies.

Dark Matter is a theory meant to explain the observed rotation of stars within a galaxy. Based upon the predicted mass of galaxies when determined from visible objects, and the movement speed of the stars around the centre, essentially there does not appear to be enough mass to gravitationally keep the stars from being flung out of galaxies, thereby “something” must account for the missing mass within galaxies.

The graph to the right plots the observed and calculated rotational speed for a typical spiral galaxy. The “calculated” curve is that predicted by taking into account the observed normal matter in the galaxy. Whereas the observed curve is the measured results.

The term Observed doesn’t only mean “light-emitting” – it also includes dark gas clouds, whose speeds we can detect by their absorption lines in the light of more distant objects, which we view through them.
