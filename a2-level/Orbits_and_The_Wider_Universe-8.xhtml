Exoplanets

We can use the mechanics of binary systems to investigate planetary systems orbiting stars other than our own sun. These planets that orbit other stars are known as exoplanets. Tremendous progress has been made during the last twenty years regarding research into this field of astronomy. One of the main concepts involved is called the Doppler Effect.

Doppler Effect

When a source of waves is moving towards an observer, the wavelength measured by the observer decreases. When a source of waves is moving away from an observer, the wavelength measured by the observer increases.

When a source of light, such as a star, is moving towards the Earth, the wavelength of the light waves received here on Earth decreases and the characteristic lines in the star’s absorption spectra shift towards the blue end of the spectrum. This is known as blue shift. If the star is moving away (receding) from Earth, the received wavelength increases and the characteristic lines in the star’s spectra shift towards the red end of the spectrum. This is known as red shift.

Remember, each line corresponds to a specific wavelength.

We can find the change in wavelength or change in frequency using  Δλ _  λ  =  v _ c  or  Δf _ f  =  v _ c  , where 𝑓 and 𝜆 are the original (unchanged) frequency and wavelength (emitted by the light source), v is the relative speed between the light source and observer, and c is the speed of light in a vacuum.

In the diagram on the right, the light source (star) is traveling with a velocity v, relative to the observer. The radial velocity, v  rad is defined as the component of the velocity acting towards the observer. In this case, v  rad = vcosθ .

In this case, the change in wavelength measured by the observer is determined using  Δλ _  λ  =   v  rad  __ c  . Here, we can see that the change in wavelength is maximum when the velocity is acting directly towards or away from the observer, and zero when the velocity is perpendicular to the observer.
