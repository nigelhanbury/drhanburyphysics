Previously, we discussed the ionisation capability of each of the 3 types of radiation, and the effect it has on the range and penetrating power. Here, we will discuss this in more depth.

The diagram on the right, represents an alpha particle passing through a material. The alpha particle is incredibly small, like the nucleus, relative to the size of the atom. Therefore, alpha particles will pass straight through the atom as shown. Since the alpha particle is positively charged, and the orbital electrons are negatively charged, there will be an electrostatic attractive force exerted on each electron the alpha particle passes close to.

This force is large enough to completely remove 1 or more orbital electrons, and ionise the atom. The diagram shows several electrons being ejected from the atom in the vicinity of the path of the alpha particle. An alpha particle travels relatively slowly (approx. 5% light speed), and carries a relatively large charge of +2e. Therefore, the alpha particle spends a relatively long period of time in the vicinity of any one atom exerting a large electrostatic force on the orbital electrons. This causes a greater number of orbital electrons to be removed as the alpha particle passes through the atom, and hence, greater ionisation of the material.

Ionisation by Alpha

The alpha particle will have transferred some of its energy to the atom in order to eject these orbital electrons, and hence, will be travelling more slowly when passing through the next atom and spend an even greater amount of time in the vicinity of the atom. This results in the alpha particle losing energy rapidly, and hence, a short distance through the material is achieved before the alpha particle loses all of its energy.

Ionisation by Beta

When a beta particle passes through the atom, it is at a significantly greater speed than that of the alpha particle, therefore, spending considerably less time in the vicinity of any one atom. Since the beta particle carries a relatively small charge of – e, a smaller electrostatic force is exerted on the orbital electrons. As a consequence, fewer orbital electrons will be removed as the beta particle passes through the atom. This results in less energy being transferred to the atom, allowing the beta particle to travel through a greater distance of the material before losing all of its energy.

Ionisation by Gamma

A gamma photon carries zero charge, and therefore, will not exert any electrostatic force on the orbital electrons as it passes through the atom. Instead, a photon can only transfer some or all of its energy when the photon directly collides with an orbital electron. This, therefore, is an extremely unlikely event. As a consequence, the gamma photon can pass through a great number of atoms before a direct collision occurs, and ejects an orbital electron. Therefore, less ionisation occurs compared to alpha and beta, coupled with gamma rays being able to pass through a much greater distance before the majority of the gamma ray energy has been transferred to the material.
