Radioactive Decay

This is the process in which an unstable nucleus (nuclide or radioisotope) spontaneously loses energy by emitting either a particle or photon, in an attempt to become more stable.

The emission of a particle or photon is known as a decay. The diagram above, represents the general process involved, where either an alpha particle, beta particle or gamma photon can be emitted. The unstable nuclide is known as the parent, and the resulting nuclide, the daughter.

It is important to note, that the result of an emission can result in the structure of the nucleus changing, and hence, the daughter nuclide can be a different element.

The 3 types of radiation we will discuss are alpha α, beta β, and gamma γ.

Alpha Decay

When a nucleus undergoes a decay, and releases an alpha particle, a group of 2 neutrons and 2 protons break away from the nucleus and are ejected at a speed of approximately 5% the speed of light. This structure can be seen on the above diagram. The charge carried by the alpha particle is equal to +2e, as the net charge carried by a single proton is +e. The neutron, of course, carries no net charge. The term ‘net charge’ is used with the appreciation that the proton and neutron are composed of quarks.

Since the alpha particle carries a large charge, and travels relatively slowly, alpha radiation is strongly ionising. As a consequence, alpha radiation cannot penetrate more than 3 - 5cm of air, and is absorbed by a sheet of paper. The structure of the alpha particle is exactly the same as a Helium nucleus, and so, can be written as 2  4  He or, in the context of alpha radiation, 2  4  𝛼 .

The nuclear decay equation can be written as  Z  A  X →  Z − 2  A − 4  Y +  2  4  𝛼 . Notice, that the mass and atomic number must be conserved. Since the atomic number is conserved, charge must be conserved.
