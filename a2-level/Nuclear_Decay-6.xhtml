Distinguishing Between Alpha, Beta and Gamma Using Absorption

The apparatus on the right, is used in distinguishing between alpha, beta, and gamma radiation.

The G-M Tube (Geiger-Muller Tube) is a common detector for all 3 types of radiation. When radiation enters the G-M Tube, ionisation occurs, which generates a series of electrical pulses. Each pulse corresponds to either an alpha or beta particle, or a gamma photon. The G-M Tube is connected to a counter, which counts the number of pulses over a fixed time interval. Each pulse detected is known as a count. The count rate detected from a particular source can then be determined. The count rate can be in terms of counts/s, counts/min or counts/hour, whichever is most appropriate.

We can make use of our knowledge of the penetrating powers of alpha, beta and gamma, in order to distinguish between them. The diagram on the right, represents the penetrating powers.

It is important to take into account background radiation, as there are many sources in the environment, which are sources of radiation. See the pie chart on the previous page. To do this, measure the count rate without any sources present.

Place a radioisotope of unknown source in front of the G-M Tube and record the count rate. To obtain the count rate due to the source, the background count rate must be subtracted.

Count rate due to source = Measured count rate – Background count rate.

Place a sheet of paper between the source and G-M Tube. If the count rate decreases significantly, then alpha particles must be emitted from the source, as the paper would block the alpha particles. If the count rate is still well above the background count, then beta and/or gamma must be present and passing through the paper. Replace the paper with several mm of Aluminium. If there is no further significant drop in the count rate, then no beta particles are emitted, otherwise, beta particles would be emitted. If the count rate is still well above background, then gamma photons must be passing through the Aluminium. Note, we are looking for significant changes in count rate as there is always statistical fluctuations of a few percent.
